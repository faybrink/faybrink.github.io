What the Buying Process Actually Looks Like

01

Buyer Consultation & Representation Agreement

Before we tour a single property, we talk through what you actually need, what you can genuinely afford, and what representation means in Texas. I ask for a signed buyer's rep agreement before sharing inventory — it protects you as much as it protects me, and it's the professional standard.

02

Pre-Approval, Not Pre-Qualification

A pre-qualification is a guess. A pre-approval is underwritten. In a market where competitive offers get accepted in days, sellers and their agents can tell the difference immediately, and it affects how your offer gets read.

03

The Search

We build a search around your actual criteria — corridor, commute, school zone, flood history, HOA restrictions — not just bedroom count and price. I flag what a listing photo won't show you before we ever schedule a showing.

04

Touring & Evaluation

I walk properties with the same systematic eye I used as an insurance adjuster — condition, deferred maintenance, and what's likely to surface on inspection — so you're evaluating the house, not just reacting to it.

05

Writing the Offer

Texas uses TREC forms. Price is one term among several — option period length, option fee, earnest money, closing date, and any repair or appraisal contingencies all affect how competitive and how protected your offer is.

06

Option Period & Inspection

Once under contract, you typically have three to ten days to have the home inspected and terminate for any reason. This window is where my adjuster background does the most work — reading the inspection report and negotiating from it.

07

Appraisal & Final Walkthrough

The lender's appraisal has to support the contract price for financing to move forward. Shortly before closing, we walk the property one more time to confirm agreed repairs were made and nothing changed since the option period.

08

Closing & Keys

Texas closings are handled by title companies, not attorneys. You sign, you fund, and keys typically change hands the same day or the next business day. Houston closings run 21 to 30 days from executed contract in most cases.

What Houston Buyers Need to Know That Most Markets Don’t

These are the factors that change the strategy in ways that don’t show up in national real estate content.

Flood Zone Due Diligence

A FEMA flood map tells you where a property technically sits, but it doesn’t tell you the street-level flooding history — particularly post-Harvey — that matters in Harris County. I check both before you fall for a house.

Harris CAD & Property Tax Proration

Property taxes are prorated at closing based on the seller’s current exemption status, which often doesn’t reflect what your bill will look like once your own homestead exemption applies the following year. Buyers who don’t plan for this get an unpleasant surprise on their first full tax bill.

New Construction vs. Resale

In Cypress, Katy, The Woodlands, and Sugar Land, builder incentives — rate buydowns, closing cost credits, design center allowances — can make new construction pencil out better than a comparable resale home, or vice versa depending on the corridor. I work both sides of that comparison daily.

HOA & Deed Restrictions

Most master-planned communities in Houston’s growth corridors carry HOA dues, architectural review requirements, and deed restrictions that affect everything from fence height to whether you can park a boat in your driveway. Reading these before closing, not after, avoids friction later.

Seasonal Patterns

Houston’s market moves with the school calendar more than most large metros. Inventory rises in spring and competition peaks before school starts. Buyers searching in the off-season often have more negotiating room than they realize.

Option Period Strategy

The option period is a buyer’s strongest protection in a Texas contract — a window to terminate for any reason for a small fee. Knowing how to use that window, and what a shorter option period costs you in a competitive offer, is a negotiation in itself.

What Buying Actually Costs

Buyers often budget for the down payment and stop there. The down payment is one line among several, and the ones that get overlooked are the ones that show up at closing as a surprise.

Earnest money is not the same as the option fee, and neither is the same as your closing costs. Conflating them is how buyers end up short at the table.

Here is what I walk every buyer through before we write an offer:

  • Earnest money — typically 1% of the purchase price, held in trust and credited toward closing if the deal goes through
  • Option fee — a small, separate, non-refundable fee paid directly to the seller for the right to terminate during the option period
  • Buyer closing costs — typically 2% to 5% of the purchase price, covering lender fees, title policy, and prepaid items like property tax and insurance escrow
  • Property tax proration — what you owe versus what the seller owes at closing, and what your first full tax bill will actually look like
  • Inspection and appraisal costs — paid out of pocket during the option period, regardless of whether the deal closes

None of these numbers are fixed until we run them for your specific price range and financing. I’d rather show you the real math up front than have you learn it at the closing table.

Buyer Questions

A buyer’s representation agreement establishes that I’m working for you — as your fiduciary — rather than simply showing you houses with no formal obligation to represent your interests. It also outlines how I’m compensated, which is now spelled out up front industry-wide rather than assumed. I ask for a signed agreement before sharing inventory because it’s the professional standard, and because it means the loyalty in the relationship is clear from the first showing.

Earnest money is a good-faith deposit, typically around 1% of the purchase price, held by the title company and applied toward your closing costs if the deal closes. The option fee is a much smaller, separate payment made directly to the seller in exchange for the right to terminate the contract for any reason during the option period. They serve different purposes and go to different places — conflating them is a common and avoidable mistake.

It depends entirely on your loan program. Conventional loans can go as low as 3% down for qualified first-time buyers, FHA loans start around 3.5%, and VA loans can require no down payment at all for eligible veterans. Twenty percent down avoids private mortgage insurance but isn’t required to buy. The right number depends on your financing, not a rule of thumb — I connect every buyer with a lender early so this gets answered with real numbers, not general ranges.

It depends on the corridor and the builder’s current incentives, which change monthly. Builders sometimes offer rate buydowns or closing cost credits that make new construction pencil out better than a comparable resale home — and sometimes resale wins because of lot premiums or upgrade costs baked into a new build. I work new construction directly and can walk you through the actual comparison for your target neighborhoods rather than a generalization.

During the option period, you can request repairs, ask for a price reduction, or terminate the contract outright for any reason, including inspection findings. What a seller agrees to depends on the market, the severity of the issue, and how the offer was structured going in. This is where reading an inspection report accurately, and knowing what’s negotiable versus what’s a genuine walk-away issue, makes the difference — it’s the part of the process my adjuster background is most directly useful for.

It varies significantly by corridor and price range, and shifts with the season. Some price points and neighborhoods still see multiple offers; others have enough inventory that buyers have real negotiating room. Rather than quote a citywide average that won’t apply to your specific search, I pull current data for your target areas before we start touring, so your offer strategy is based on what’s actually happening where you’re looking.
