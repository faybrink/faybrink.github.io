## 1. blog-divorce-stalling-spouse-sale.html
**New paragraph** after "What actually gets a stalled sale moving."

**What stalling can cost the spouse doing it.** This is the part a stalling spouse rarely thinks through. Once a decree or court order clearly says the house gets sold (a move-out date, who lists it, a deadline to sign), refusing to leave, blocking showings, or sitting on paperwork can land that spouse back in front of the judge on a contempt motion. Texas courts can enforce a property division by contempt when the order requires handing over specific property, and they have other tools too: further orders spelling out exactly how the sale happens, a receiver appointed to take the sale out of both spouses' hands, and attorney's fees awarded against the spouse who caused the delay. Every one of those costs money, and much of it comes out of the proceeds. Meanwhile the mortgage, taxes, insurance, and HOA keep running, the house shows in whatever shape the occupying spouse keeps it, and buyers can tell a contested, court-supervised sale when they walk in. The well-marketed price both spouses could have had turns into a rushed one that's worse for both of them, including the one who dug in. Whether your decree is specific enough to be enforced that way is a question for your attorney, because the wording matters.

## 2. blog-avoid-court-ordered-home-sale-divorce.html
**New section** placed before "Where a Realtor Fits Into Avoiding a Forced Sale."

### When One Spouse Ignores the Order
A court-ordered sale doesn't end the problem if one spouse won't cooperate with it. When the order is clear about what has to happen and by when, a spouse who refuses to move out, won't allow showings, or won't sign can be held in contempt. The court can also issue more detailed orders, appoint a receiver to run the sale, and make the uncooperative spouse pay the other side's attorney's fees. If the decree is vague, the court usually has to clarify it and give a reasonable window to comply before contempt is on the table. So how your order is written matters, and that's worth going over with your attorney before the decree is final.

The part people miss is what all of that does to the price. A receiver's fees, extra months of mortgage and carrying costs, a house shown while someone is fighting the sale, and a timeline set by the court instead of the market all eat into the proceeds. A home that would have brought a strong price with a cooperative, well-marketed listing can end up selling for less. Both spouses split the smaller number, including the one who caused the delay.

**Added to the end of the answer** to "What if one spouse is living in the house and won't leave?"
